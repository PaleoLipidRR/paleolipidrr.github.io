Archaeal lipids track AOA ecology and evolution

The GDGT-2/GDGT-3 ratio separates shallow and deep ecotypes of marine ammonia-oxidizing archaea, a fingerprint we trace back to the Mesozoic.

Published

R. Rattanasriampaipong, Y. G. Zhang, A. Pearson, B. P. Hedlund, S. Zhang

Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 119 · 2022

Paper PDF

In brief

Archaeal membrane lipids (GDGTs) are best known as a paleothermometer, but they also carry a record of which archaea made them. By comparing published GDGTs from laboratory cultures of marine and hot-spring archaea, modern seawater and sediments, and ancient sediments up to 192 million years old, we separate the “thermal” behavior of these lipids from an ecological fingerprint of shallow- and deep-water ammonia-oxidizing archaea (AOA).

Key findings

  • A presumed temperature-dependent (PTD) trend. If temperature alone controlled lipid synthesis, TEX86 would rise and GDGT-2/GDGT-3 would fall as water warms. Cultured AOA and shallow-water samples follow that trend.
  • Shallow vs. deep ecotypes. Samples from below the pycnocline break away from the PTD trend: deep AOA raise GDGT-2/GDGT-3 and blur its link to temperature.
  • Gaussian mixture models turn these patterns into statistical fingerprints of AOA ecology that can be applied to paleo-GDGT records.
  • AOA niche expansion. Long-term GDGT-2/GDGT-3 trends suggest today’s deep-water AOA were suppressed during Mesozoic–early Cenozoic greenhouse climates and expanded with later oceanographic and climate change.
Distribution patterns of archaeal membrane lipids across different archives. The ‘presumed thermal dependent’ trend is shown by the shaded gray bars, where higher TEX86 goes with lower GDGT-2/GDGT-3 ratios.

From finding to predictor → TEXAS

GDGT-2/GDGT-3 became one of the two nonthermal predictors in TEXAS, our proxy system model for TEX86 paleothermometry, where it accounts for lipids from deep-water archaea. The glacial–interglacial case study at MD98-2152 and U1482 shows it at work.

    Archaeal lipids trace ecology and evolution of marine ammonia-oxidizing archaea
    Ronnakrit Rattanasriampaipong, Yi Ge Zhang, Ann Pearson, Brian P Hedlund, and Shuang Zhang
    Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2022
    Abs DOI Bib PDF

    Archaeal membrane lipids are widely used for paleotemperature reconstructions, yet these molecular fossils also bear rich information about ecology and evolution of marine ammonia-oxidizing archaea (AOA). Here we identified ‘thermal’ and ‘non-thermal’ behaviors of archaeal glycerol dialkyl glycerol tetraethers (GDGTs) by comparing the GDGT-based temperature index (TEX86) to the ratio of GDGTs with two and three cyclopentane rings (GDGT-2/GDGT-3). Thermal-dependent biosynthesis should increase TEX86 and decrease GDGT-2/GDGT-3 when the ambient temperature increases. This presumed temperature-dependent (PTD) trend is observed in GDGTs derived from cultures of thermophilic and mesophilic AOA. The distribution of GDGTs in suspended particulate matter (SPM) and sediments collected from above the pycnocline— ‘shallow-water’ samples—also follows the PTD trend. These similar GDGT distributions between AOA cultures and shallow-water environmental samples reflect ‘shallow’ ecotypes of marine AOA. While there are currently no cultures of ‘deep’ AOA clades, GDGTs derived from ‘deep-water’ SPM and marine sediment samples exhibit non-thermal behavior deviating from the PTD trend. The presence of ‘deep’ AOA increases the GDGT-2/GDGT-3 ratio and distorts the temperature-controlled correlation between GDGT-2/GDGT-3 and TEX86. We used Gaussian Mixture Models to statistically characterize these diagnostic patterns of modern AOA ecology from paleo-GDGT records to infer the evolution of marine AOA from the Mid-Mesozoic to the present. Long-term GDGT-2/GDGT-3 trends suggest the suppression of today’s ‘deep-water’ marine AOA during the Mesozoic–early Cenozoic greenhouse climates. Our analysis provides insights into the evolutionary timeline and the expansion of AOA niches associated with major oceanographic and climate changes.
